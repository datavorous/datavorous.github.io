summary of the proposed framework:

==========================

cycle 1

1. 100 new attendees show up
2. gather data on what have you built, what have you broken, 
    what made you join. nothing fancy, just enough to seed groups
3. one session, navigating ambiguity (whiteboard+ live problem)
4. split into groups of 5, seeded with one person who has some pulse. 
    two nudgers per group.
5. each group pick one project from two options: one familiar, one niche
6. weekly CF contest starts, results posted publicly
7. two weeks of work, seniors stay out unless asked
8. week 3, groups present to each other. find flaws, iterate 
    (pick random pairs of 2 groups)
9. every group pushes one public artifact

maybe 60 submit something. 20 have a repo. 8 want to continue.

cycle 2

1. new project menu, new niche option
2. the 8 consistent ones anchor new groups
3. CF contest continues, ratings start showing movement
4. same structure, no new roles added

cycle 3 onwards

the group shrinks to its real size. maybe 10-15. 
output is visible and public. that's the club.

==========================

possible shortcomings:
0. some people start for the very first time
    rebuttal: cycle 1 will be of longer duration, and cycle(1).point(4) nudgers act like unblockers
1. cycle(1).point(5) - if we want interdisciplinery fields to infuse, we need volunteers from different branches
    rebuttal: we start dead simple, with an aim to replicate little tools used in any particular field
        ex: k-mer analyser for  FASTA files. 
2. sustaining headcount is a tough thing
    rebuttal: inclusitivity

==========================

based on the following theories of group dynamics:

1. cynefin framework (created at IBM in 1999)

categorises problems into 5 domains: clear, complicated, complex, chaotic, confused.
the things which we aspire to do: systems programming, ml systems, cybersec etc.
these will live in the complex domain. nobody can tell you the right way to reverse engineer 
a compiler or optimise a triton kernel. one needs to probe themselves, observe what breaks and then 
learn from what broke to try again with a different hypothesis. lectures fail here, they assume
the domain is clear or complicated which they aint.

2. solo vs group vs group-on-group 

solo problem solving has its own fault, main one being lack of external perspective
which results in a higher rate of quitting.

in groups, we got multiple probes happening simultaneously on the same problem 
here shared context can reduce redundant dead ends. 

group on group: we now have 2 independent probes of the same sapce that diverged 
in different directions. group X built something in one way, group Y build it 
in a different way. when they present, the divergence is the learning. 
why did they diverge? which approach held under what constraint? 

3. tuckman's stages: every group goes through forming, storming, norming, performing.
now enforced with a time limit cycle(timelimit), groups that cant move through storming fast enough self eliminate
before wasting resources 

4. last but not the least, we have the hackman's team effectiveness model:
conditions matter more than who is in the team. 
a. real boundaries
b. compelling direction
c. right structure -> enforced by seeded groups.
